Assertive communication

Assertive communication is direct, clear, and respectful. It is not aggressive (it doesn't bulldoze or dismiss), and it is not passive (it doesn't hint or hedge). It states clearly what you think, what you need, and what you expect, while remaining open to other perspectives.

A founder who softens every message to avoid discomfort trains their team to read between the lines. A founder who delivers hard feedback in a way that feels like an attack trains their team to go quiet. The goal is to be the kind of communicator your team can trust to say what they mean and mean what they say.

Active listening

Active listening is where most founders have the most room to grow. In a fast-moving startup, the instinct is always toward action and output, but active listening prevents misalignment from compounding.

In practice: ask follow-up questions before offering solutions, confirm what you heard before redirecting the conversation, and stay open to the possibility that the other person's experience of a situation may differ from your own. Your Coach sessions are a built-in opportunity to practise this in a safe, structured setting.

Choosing the right channel

Choosing the wrong channel for a message is itself a communication failure. It creates noise, slows response, and signals a lack of intentionality.

Real-time conversation (in person or video) is best for anything emotionally complex or relationship-sensitive: feedback conversations, difficult decisions, sensitive updates.

Asynchronous written communication (email, shared documents, recorded updates) is best for information that needs to be referenced later or needs a paper trail.

Chat tools are best for quick coordination and informal exchange, not for anything that needs nuance, depth, or documentation.

Communication frameworks

When a conversation is high-stakes (difficult feedback, a conflict, a significant change), a framework can help you avoid the patterns that tend to derail it.

SBI (Situation, Behaviour, Impact) is the go-to framework for feedback. Describe the specific situation, name the observable behaviour (not your interpretation of it), and explain the impact it had on the team, the work, or the business.

SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is useful for communicating a complex issue efficiently, to your team, an investor, or in a crisis. State the situation, provide context, share your analysis, and make a clear recommendation.
